Every CTA on this page leaves the page

The traffic strategy is sound and the audience targeting is unusually sharp. What follows the click is a page that describes a category rather than selling a vendor, and a CTA system that routes qualified visitors away from the only context that qualifies them.

The core problem

This page is built to rank, not to close. It explains what a trading platform is to an audience that already runs one, lists 46 capabilities without making a single argument for itexus over a competitor, and then hands the visitor a form.

The buyer it names — a hedge fund, a brokerage, an FCM — is the most risk-averse buyer in software. They are not asking “what features does a trading platform have.” They are asking “can this firm be trusted with order flow, and what happens to me if they can't.” In 1,779 words the page answers neither.

The CTA system routes away from itself

Five of six calls to action navigate to /contacts/ — a generic company-wide form. The page's own contact form, which is better than the destination, sits at 86% depth and is reachable only by scrolling.

The destination is worse than the origin

/contacts/ carries the identical five fields, no calendar, no trading context, and — critically — none of the four-step reassurance block (“24 hours · NDA signed · contract in 1–2 weeks · MVP live in 3–4 months”) that makes the landing page's own form persuasive. It does add one thing the landing page lacks: an “NDA Required” checkbox, which is exactly the risk-reversal control this audience wants and it is on the wrong page.

So a visitor who clicks the hero CTA in the first five seconds is moved from a page that has just qualified them into a generic form that has forgotten who they are, with less reassurance and a fresh page load.

“Book” is a promise the page doesn't keep

“Book a consultation” and “Book a Free Consultation” use calendar language. There is no calendar — no embed, no slots, no named person, no duration. The visitor braces for a two-click booking and gets a five-field form and an unspecified wait. Either install a real scheduler and keep the verb, or change the verb to match what happens.

Fifty exits from the body copy

The main content contains 50 links leaving the page, several placed at the highest-intent moments. The FAQ answer to “How much does trading platform development cost?” — the single most commercially loaded question on the page — resolves to “there is no fixed cost” and a link to a blog post. The reader's cost question is answered by sending them somewhere else.

Message-match breaks at the door

The meta description — the promise made in the search result — reads: “…aligned with your business goals and built for traders. 300+ Projects, Ph.D. level engineers.”

Neither claim appears anywhere on the page. Searching the full 1,779 words for 300, Ph.D, PhD, engineers, or years returns zero matches. The strongest credibility the company advertises evaporates on arrival, and the visitor's first screen carries 30 words, one CTA, and no reason to believe anything.

The page title is also pure keyword — “Trading Platform Development Services” — repeated verbatim as the H1. That is a wasted asset twice: no differentiator in the SERP snippet to win the click, and no argument in the H1 to hold the visit.

A due-diligence checklist for a brokerage or fund evaluating a build partner, scored against the current page.

Three trading case studies, all “Under NDA,” all described by feature rather than outcome. The featured one is a crypto wallet, not a trading platform.

The four-step process block states 24 hours, NDA, contract in 1–2 weeks, MVP in 3–4 months. Concrete, dated, credible — the strongest content on the page.

Answered well

Seven of eight due-diligence questions go unanswered on a page whose stated audience cannot sign a contract without answering them. Every one of these is content the company already has internally — this is a publishing gap, not a capability gap.

The page has one offer, and it is the most expensive one

Every conversion path on the page terminates in the same high-commitment action: talk to sales. For a purchase with a 3–4 month delivery and a six-figure price, the share of visitors ready for that on first visit is small. Everyone else — the architect doing vendor research, the CTO building a shortlist, the founder scoping a budget — has nothing to convert into.

There is no second rung on the ladder. No cost model, no reference architecture, no build-vs-buy comparison, no compliance checklist, no sample proposal, no benchmark report, no newsletter. The page captures in-market buyers and loses the entire research-stage audience it worked hard to attract, at exactly the moment they were most interested.

The most obvious asset is already written and living on the blog: the trading-system cost breakdown the FAQ links to. Gate a deeper version of it on the page rather than sending the reader to a blog post that has no CTA of its own.

The pattern repeats through all four capability sections: the page describes what a trading platform contains, addressed to someone learning the category, while claiming to speak to hedge funds and exchanges who built one already. The result reads as a glossary with a form attached.

The exception proves the point. “We help select and integrate brokers based on market coverage, API quality, latency, and SLA” is the best sentence on the page, because it is the only one that names a decision the buyer is actually struggling with rather than a component they already know exists. Write the other forty-five that way, or cut them.

Two FAQ entries actively serve the wrong reader. “What does trading software do?” defines the category to someone buying custom development of it. “What is the most used trading software?” answers with MetaTrader 4, TradingView and Thinkorswim — the page spends a paragraph recommending three competing products to a prospect. Both belong on the blog, where they can earn informational traffic without diluting a sales page.

Form friction

Five visible fields plus consent, on a page where the reader has been given no reason to trust the recipient. Beyond the accessibility defects covered in the design critique, three conversion changes:

  • Cut to three fields. Name, work email, and “what are you building.” Company can be inferred from the email domain; phone is the field people abandon on, and you can ask for it in the reply.
  • Move the NDA checkbox here. It exists on /contacts/ and not on the page that actually needs it. For this audience, “NDA required” next to the submit button is the single highest-value control you can add.
  • Set the expectation at the button, not beside it. “Get in touch” says nothing. “Send — reply within 24 hours” restates the commitment at the exact moment the reader decides.

Also worth testing: the four-step process block currently sits beside the form on desktop but above it on mobile, where it pushes the fields to 84% depth behind four paragraphs of explanation. On mobile, put the fields first and the process below them.

Where to start

  1. Point the CTAs at the page's own form

    Change the five off-page CTAs to an anchor scroll to the on-page form, and move the NDA checkbox over from /contacts/. This is a href change and a checkbox, and it stops the page discarding its own qualifying context on every click.

    Hours of work · touches every conversion path

  2. Put proof in the first screen

    Add the proof strip — projects, years, Clutch rating, NDA-first — and rewrite the H1 as an outcome rather than a repeat of the page title. The claims already exist in the meta description and the footer; they are simply not where the visitor can see them.

    Days of work · fixes the message-match break

  3. Answer the cost question on the page

    Replace “there is no fixed cost” with a discovery price and two or three engagement bands. A range qualifies out the visitors who were never going to buy and reassures the ones who were; the current answer does neither and sends both to the blog.

    A pricing decision, then an afternoon

  4. Add the second offer

    Gate a deeper version of the cost breakdown you already publish, and put it behind the secondary hero CTA and a mid-page inline capture. This is the only change that converts the research-stage traffic the page is currently attracting and losing.

    One asset · captures the 90% not ready to talk

  5. Rebuild proof and add the due-diligence content

    Anonymised outcome numbers on the three trading case studies, a security and compliance section, a named architect with a stack, and explicit risk-reversal terms. Then cut the four capability lists to one. This is the largest job and the one that decides whether a brokerage can actually shortlist you.
